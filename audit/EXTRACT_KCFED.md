# Extraction: Cook, Kazinnik, Modig & Palmer, "What Do LLMs Want?"

Source: `research/notes/what-do-llms-want-feds-2026-006-full-text.md` (full-text extraction of FEDS 2026-006 / KC Fed RWP 25-19). All section/table/figure numbers below are the paper's own numbering. Quotes are verbatim from the extracted text unless marked paraphrase.

## (1) The scaling claim, precisely

**Models and parameter counts (Table 1, "Models Examined"):**

| Developer | Model | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Mistral | Mistral v0.3 | 7B (7.3B stated in prose) |
| Mistral | Mistral Small 3.1 | 24B |
| Mistral | Mistral Small 3.2 | 24B |
| Microsoft | Phi 4 | 14B |
| Microsoft | Phi 4 Reasoning | 14B |
| Microsoft | Phi 4 Reasoning Plus | 14B |
| Google | Gemma 3 | 27B |
| AllenAI | OLMo 2 | 13B |
| Meta | LLaMA 4 Maverick | 17B × 128E (~400B effective, MoE) |
| Meta | LLaMA 4 Scout | 17B × 16E (~109B effective, MoE) |

Llama 4 Maverick/Scout are evaluated only "in a limited set of exercises" (excluded from most McCall/persona/control-vector regressions) because of their size/complexity.

**The rationalizability metric is NOT GARP.** It is a bespoke three-part consistency test defined for the McCall job-search environment (Section 4.3), built on a fitted switching-regime (change-point) model, not on Afriat/GARP revealed-preference axioms. (GARP/Afriat language appears only in Section 1.1 as the paper's general theoretical framing and in the Fehr-Schmidt dictator-game discussion, where the authors describe dictator-game behavior as "well rationalized by the Fehr-Schmidt model" — a different, non-GARP sense of "rationalized.")

The McCall rationalizability procedure, in the paper's own words:

- For well-ordered preferences the paper expects a step function: "yi,j(w) = Accept if w > w̄, Reject if w < w̄" (Eq. 11), i.e., a single reservation wage w̄.
- Behavior is modeled per experiment as "two Bernoulli regimes separated by a switch-point," giving three parameters — switch-point τ and acceptance probabilities p0, p1 above/below it — estimated by maximum likelihood, against a null "no switch-point" one-parameter model.
- **Three criteria, all of which must pass for a reservation wage w̄ to be said to "exist" for that experiment:**
  1. "Switching is selected: Does the BIC criteria select the switching model? If not, then the best description of the data is random choice; reject that w̄ exists."
  2. "Step up: In the switching model, is p0 < p1? Does the step function 'step up?' If not, reject that w̄ exists."
  3. "Trembling hand: In the switching model, are the trembling hand errors each less than 50%? If one is greater than 50%, reject that w̄ exists." (Footnote: added "largely due to author taste.")
- If w̄ is found to exist, the paper then asks whether a discount factor β can be numerically estimated such that v*(w > w̄) > v*(w < w̄) under the McCall value function (Section 4.3.1). "Even at this stage, not every w̄ has an associated β that is rationalized by the McCall model... All models display some fraction of instances in which there does not exist a β that rationalizes the w̄ selected by the model."
- So "rationalizable" for a given model/experiment/prompt is a **binary pass/fail** on this compound test, and the paper reports the population fraction of experiments passing at each stage (Figures 11, 13, 14) rather than a single scalar score.

**Numeric results.** The paper reports these fractions only as **plotted figures** (Figure 11: fraction of accept-below-b errors vs. model size; Figure 13: fraction of instances w̄ exists vs. model size; Figure 14: fraction of instances β exists vs. model size; Figure 15/16: mean/SD of estimated β vs. model size), not as a printed numeric table — the extracted full text does not contain digit-level percentages for these figures (they are only visible as chart axes/points in the PDF, not as OCR'd table cells). The prose descriptions accompanying the figures are:

- Fig. 11 (accept-when-w<b, lower=better): "Larger models tend to fair better... the smallest model, Mistral v0.3 7B, has some trouble accepting too many offers, while the second-smallest, OLMo 2, accepts too few... The reasoning versions of Phi 4 do worse than Phi 4 non-reasoning, a theme we will see repeated."
- Fig. 13 (fraction w̄ exists, higher=better): "Bigger models do better than smaller models, sometimes dramatically so. The largest model, Gemma 3, produces a policy with an appropriate reservation wage nearly all the time for all prompts. The Mistral Small models follow close behind, and the Phi 4 non-reasoning does quite well, even at 50–60% the size of the larger Mistral Small and Gemma 3 models. The Phi 4 reasoning models do worse for some prompts, and OLMo2 has very high variance across prompts, all lower than equivalent prompts for larger models. Mistral v0.3 has the least success."
- Fig. 14 (fraction β exists): "The same patterns hold as previously: the largest models do the best, Phi 4 non-reasoning does better than the reasoning models, and the smallest model essentially has no rationalizable behavior with respect to any version of the McCall model tested."
- Summary sentence (verbatim): **"To summarize, large models are rationalizable within the McCall framework, while the smallest model is not rationalizable at all."**
- Estimated β: "the average β falls between 0.2 and 0.8 across all models," with within-model/prompt SD "of 0.2–0.3" — i.e., these discount factors "do not align well with traditional estimates of human discount factors."

So the prior summary's claims are directionally correct but the paper does not supply exact per-model percentages in text — only qualitative rank-orderings from figures. There is no single named "rationalizability metric" with one published number per model; it is the three-stage w̄-existence test above, reported as a rate across many (model × prompt × experiment) trials.

**Reasoning-variant claim — confirmed, with the two loci where it appears:**
1. Figure 11 (accept-below-b errors): "The reasoning versions of Phi 4 do worse than Phi 4 non-reasoning."
2. Figure 12 (policy-function case study): "The Phi 4 Reasoning Plus model in the bottom left does worse than its non-reasoning counterpart in the top left, making many 'trembling hand' errors."
3. Figure 13/14 fraction-exists plots: "Phi 4 non-reasoning does quite well... The Phi 4 reasoning models do worse for some prompts" / "Phi 4 non-reasoning does better than the reasoning models."

No numeric magnitude is given for the gap — only the qualitative ranking Phi 4 (base) > Phi 4 Reasoning ≈ Phi 4 Reasoning Plus, repeated across three separate figures/analyses.

## (2) The framing/reframing result

**Three steering mechanisms, named exactly as the paper names them (Section 1, abstract, and Sections 3.4–3.6, 4.4–4.5):**
1. **Personas** ("Mitigation via personas," §3.4/§4.5) — the model is instructed to "assume the role of" a synthetic persona drawn from NVIDIA's Nemotron-Personas dataset (60 sampled personas, 22-field demographic/text profiles).
2. **Prompt masking** ("Mitigation via Prompt Masking," §3.5/§4.4) — "we recontextualize a problem so that its description seems different than its canonical form while leaving the underlying mathematical structure of it intact," e.g. masking the dictator game as a FOREX trade, currency exchange, or landlord/rent-reduction scenario, and masking the McCall search problem as a "Market Game" or "Asset Game" (goods/financial-instrument trading framings) to obscure that it is the classic McCall problem.
3. **Control vectors** ("Mitigation via Control Vectors," §3.6) — representation-level steering (see item 3 below).

The paper's own framing sentence (abstract/intro, verbatim): "context reframing (e.g., masking social context) and control vectors reliably shift models toward more payoff-maximizing behavior, while persona-based prompting has more limited impact." Also: "when we mask the task's economic context by reframing it, allocations shift toward self-interest."

**Effect sizes, reframing vs. persona (Section 3, dictator game):**
- *Persona effect (Table 6, "Dictator Game Results with Personas," persona-fixed-effects regression, N=25,000 for most models):* R² = 0.025 (Mistral v0.3), **0.648 (Gemma 3)**, 0.036 (Olmo 2), 0.037 (Phi-4), 0.009 (Phi-4 Reasoning), 0.011 (Phi-4 Reasoning Plus), 0.056 (Mistral Small 3.2). The paper: "The effectiveness of personas is model dependent... only a small percent of the variation in model response for all other models [besides Gemma 3]." In the McCall setting personas fare even worse (Table 9 R² values as low as 0.013): "we did not find that personas had a meaningful impact on LLM behavior... even Gemma 3, which did respond strongly to the use of personas in the dictator game exercise, did not respond to personas when applied in this setting."
- *Prompt-masking effect:* Reported qualitatively rather than as a single comparable R²/coefficient figure ("Figure 7 and Figure 8 show the effectiveness of the prompt masking in reorienting models towards the self-interest optimizing behavior... it does generally appear to be the case that prompt masks do meaningfully steer the responses generated by the LLM"; for the McCall exercise, "framing the scenario as an employment search problem tends to make LLMs considerably more patient" per Tables 7–8 coefficient estimates, which are statistically significant (starred) for most model/prompt-mask combinations — see the regression coefficients extracted above under Table 7). Prompt masking is described as working "at least in part because the masking redirects the model away from its knowledge of the academic literature on dictator and ultimatum type games," and its magnitude was large enough that Llama 4 (Maverick/Scout) responses to the FOREX/currency-exchange masks "collapse to 0.0."
- No apples-to-apples statistical test (e.g., same R² metric) directly compares masking-effect-size vs. persona-effect-size in the extracted text; the comparison is qualitative ("prompt masking does shift LLM responses significantly and in a predictable direction" vs. personas "did not consistently allow us to steer LLM behavior").

**Is the reframing effect reported separately by model scale? — No, not directly, and the extracted text says so implicitly.** Section 4.4 (McCall masking) discusses the effect only in aggregate/model-by-model prose, not scaled or plotted against parameter count the way Figures 11/13/14 (rationalizability) are. The one scale-relevant remark is a caveat, not a quantified scale interaction: "we could characterize the latent patience of most models as being lower and elevated by their knowledge of McCall. Exceptions to this would be OLMo 2 and Phi 4, which would appear to have higher latent patience that is brought lower by their knowledge of the literature on McCall." This shows *model-specific* heterogeneity in masking response, not a monotonic-with-scale relationship, and the paper explicitly flags interpretive uncertainty here ("There may, of course, be other interpretations for the influence of prompt masking. Further analysis is required..."). **The decisive question — does reframing move large models as much as small ones — is not directly answered by any figure or regression comparing masking-coefficient magnitude against parameter count.** The persona-side scale finding is clearer but goes the opposite direction expected: persona malleability is concentrated almost entirely in the *largest* model in the roster (Gemma 3, 27B, R²=0.648) while all mid/small models show weak persona effects (R² ≤ 0.056) — i.e., susceptibility to persona steering does not decrease with scale in this data; if anything the one large-effect case is the largest model tested.

## (3) The control-vector intervention

**Mechanics (Section 3.6, based on Cook and Kazinnik 2025 and Zou et al. 2023):** For a model represented as a sequence of layer-wise transformations gk(x) = fk(gk−1(x)), the internal representation vector v(x) = [g1(x),...,gK−1(x)] is computed for paired "contrasting pair prompts" (CPPs) D = {(xi, yi)} that differ only in a targeted concept — here, xi contains language emphasizing "rationally optimizing" self-interest, yi an inversion emphasizing "fair and equitable" preferences (all CPPs built on the FOREX dictator-game mask, chosen because it produced the most self-interest-convergent baseline responses). The difference vector Δ(x,y) = v(x) − v(y) is taken as the concept direction. It is applied at inference by adding it, scaled by coefficient s, into each layer's forward pass: g'_k = fk(g_{k-1} + sΔ(x,y)), producing shifted output G' = g'_K.

**Continuous strength parameter:** Yes — s is continuous and swept across a range of values ("varying levels of strength of the coefficient," "varying levels of the coefficient s"). Negative s orients toward "fairness"/equity; positive s orients toward self-interest. The extracted text does **not** state the exact number of discrete s levels tested or their numeric range — this information exists only in the (non-OCR'd) axes of Figures 9, 10, 18, and 19, not as text/table values. Footnote 10 does specify sampling density: "For each value of coefficient shown, approximately 100 LLM responses were sampled. Samples were taken individually and caching was disabled."

**Outcome measure:** The offer proportion p (dictator/FOREX game, continuous 0.0–1.0, self-reported by the model as its "action" field) or the accept/reject decision (McCall variants — control vectors are applied only to the dictator/FOREX game in the extracted material, not reported for McCall). **This outcome measure is NOT exogenous.** It is the LLM's own self-reported choice variable in a single-shot, non-repeated game with no real counterparty and no realized payment — there is no external ground-truth payoff being measured independently of the model's stated preference judgment. The paper's own description confirms this: control vectors are evaluated by whether "application of the control vector at higher levels tended to move LLM responses towards self-interest maximizing offers of p=0.0" and "values of the coefficient below zero tended to shift the LLM responses... in favor of more equitable offers of p=0.5" — i.e., the outcome is explicitly framed as movement of the response distribution "toward the theory-predicted direction" (self-interest-maximization or equity), not a measurement of an independently realized payoff. Notably even Gemma 3, whose baseline dictator-game responses "demonstrated effectively no inequality aversion," "could be pushed towards egalitarian offers when s was pushed to extreme levels" — again framed as movement in the predicted direction, not as a payoff outcome.

## (4) Model roster and open-weight/self-hostable overlap

**Full roster (10 models/variants, all open-weight):** Mistral v0.3 (7B), Mistral Small 3.1 (24B), Mistral Small 3.2 (24B), Phi 4 (14B), Phi 4 Reasoning (14B), Phi 4 Reasoning Plus (14B), Gemma 3 (27B), OLMo 2 (13B), LLaMA 4 Maverick (17B×128E MoE), LLaMA 4 Scout (17B×16E MoE).

**Explicitly excluded, by design (Table 1, footnote 3, verbatim):** "We exclude proprietary models such as GPT-4 or [vendor-assistant] due to their closed-weight nature and limited reproducibility. Our focus is on models that can be downloaded and run independently to support transparent, replicable experimentation." Section 2 intro: "we evaluate a set of open-weight large language models, focusing on those that are freely available, reproducible, and deployable using modest hardware resources (e.g., a single GPU or local server)."

**Open-weight and realistically self-hostable on consumer hardware:**
- **Mistral v0.3 7B** — yes, directly comparable to Llama 3.x 8B / Qwen2.5 7B class; runs on a single consumer GPU.
- **Phi 4 / Phi 4 Reasoning / Phi 4 Reasoning Plus (14B)** — yes, runnable on a single consumer/prosumer GPU (comparable class to Phi-3.5 mentioned in the audit brief, one generation newer).
- **OLMo 2 (13B)** — yes, fully open (weights, training code, and data), comparable hardware footprint to the above.
- **Gemma 3 (27B)** and **Mistral Small 3.1/3.2 (24B)** — open-weight but heavier; the paper itself notes Mistral Small runs "efficiently on a single 80GB GPU," i.e., feasible on a high-end workstation/single large GPU (e.g., A100/H100-class or a well-specced consumer card with quantization) but not "modest hardware" in the same sense as the 7B–14B tier.
- **LLaMA 4 Maverick / Scout** — open-weight in principle but MoE models with ~109B–400B effective parameters; **not realistically self-hostable on consumer hardware**, and the paper itself only evaluates them "in a limited set of exercises" for this reason.

**Direct overlap with the families named in the audit brief:** No exact-checkpoint overlap with Llama 3.x 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, or Phi-3.5, since the paper uses Mistral v0.3 7B, Phi 4 (not 3.5), and Gemma 3 (not stated whether 3 vs. a numbered sub-variant) rather than those specific checkpoints. But there is **family-level overlap**: Gemma (family), Phi (family, one version ahead), and Mistral (family) are all directly represented, and Llama is represented only at non-locally-hostable MoE scale (Llama 4, not 3.x). A locally-hosted pilot using Llama 3.x 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, Mistral 7B, Phi-3.5, and Gemma would share **family** identity with 3 of the paper's 4 non-Llama families (Mistral, Phi, Gemma) but no identical checkpoints, and would add Llama 3.x/Qwen2.5 coverage the paper does not have at a locally-hostable scale.

## (5) Design details

- **Dictator/pie/ultimatum exercise:** Single continuous choice variable p ∈ [0,1] (dictator/pie game "action" field, self-reported as a JSON float) or a monetary pot split — this is a **single-good, single-decision allocation task** (one pot, two accounts), not a multi-good consumption-bundle/budget design. N = **120 observations per model** for the main dictator-game regressions (Table 3/4; Llama 4 models also N=120) and **N = 117–119 per model** for the pie-game regressions (Table 2). Pot size ("pot") is a template variable varied "randomly with each prompt" (example values seen in prompts: $510, $201,000, $1,000,000); the paper states pot size has no meaningful effect on responses ("the size of the pot to be divided does not meaningfully influence the response of any of the LLMs") but the extracted text does not give the exact numeric distribution (e.g., uniform/log-uniform range) or precision used to draw pot sizes — this detail is not present in the OCR'd text (likely only in an appendix table/figure not captured, or specified only in code).
- **Persona exercise:** 60 unique personas sampled from NVIDIA's Nemotron-Personas dataset, crossed with prompt-location (system vs. user message) and phrasing variants, applied to the dictator game (N=25,000 observations for most models in Table 6) and to the McCall game (N ranging 1,382–13,212 across models in Table 9).
- **McCall search exercise:** Single continuous choice-relevant variable is the wage/price offer w (accept/reject binary decision per offer), governed by four economic parameters per "experiment": b (unemployment benefit / reserve price / dividend), μw and σw (mean/SD of the offer distribution), and pe (per-period probability the process ends). Each "experiment" = one draw of (b, σw, μw, pe) plus an offer schedule W = {w} and a prompt mask; the model produces accept/reject decisions for each offer in W. Figure 12's case-study panels each display "10 experiments" per model/prompt combination. Observation counts in the post-hoc regression tables (Table 7/8/9 "Observations" rows, i.e. after the w̄-existence filter) range from **186 to 874 per model** (masking regressions) and 1,382–13,212 per model (persona regressions) — these reflect surviving/aggregated trials, not the raw offer-grid size. The exact number of distinct wage offers per experiment, and the numeric precision/distribution used to draw b, μw, σw, pe, are **not stated in the extracted text** (prompts show these as template placeholders {b}, {μw}, {σw}, {pe} without disclosed sampling ranges).
- **Budget exhaustion:** Not applicable in the GARP/consumption-bundle sense — there is no multi-good budget line being exhausted. In the dictator game the "budget" (pot) is always fully allocated between the two players by construction (p and 1−p sum to the whole pot); in the McCall game there is no budget constraint at all, only a sequential accept/reject decision over an income stream.

## Direct relevance to a locally-hosted pilot

Every model in this paper is open-weight and explicitly chosen for reproducibility on modest hardware (proprietary models are explicitly excluded, footnote 3). Three of the four non-Llama families (Mistral, Phi, Gemma) are directly represented at locally-runnable 7B–27B scale, giving family-level (not checkpoint-identical) comparability to a pilot using Llama 3.x 8B, Qwen2.5 7B, Mistral 7B, Phi-3.5, and Gemma. The paper's own scale finding — larger models pass its three-part McCall w̄-existence test more often, and Phi 4's reasoning variants underperform its base variant — is reported only as qualitative figure-based rankings, not printed percentages, so a pilot replicating this claim numerically would be filling a genuine reporting gap rather than duplicating published numbers. The reframing-vs-persona and reframing-by-scale questions are also not cleanly answered in text, leaving room for a pilot to test them directly.
