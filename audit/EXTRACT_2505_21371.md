## (a) Model roster and scale

Four models, tested in Case Study 1 (budgetary/CCEI tasks) and Case Study 2 (behavioral games):

| Model | Paper's own description (verbatim) | Parameter count given in paper |
|---|---|---|
| GPT-4o-2024-11-20 ("GPT") | "the advanced LLM developed by OpenAI, which is widely regarded as the pioneering leader in the field" | Not given for this exact checkpoint. Background Table S4 lists OpenAI's disclosed parameter counts (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, GPT-4o-mini, o3) as **"Unknown"** — GPT-4o itself is not even a row in that table. |
| DeepSeek-V3-0324 ("DeepSeek") | "the open-sourced model with the largest parameter size [65]" | Not stated for the -0324 checkpoint specifically, but background Table S4 lists "DeepSeek-V3" (the base model family) at **671B** parameters, MoE architecture. |
| Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct ("Llama") | "a widely studied model in artificial intelligence and the most downloaded text-generation model released since 2024, ranked by Hugging Face" | **8B** (from Table S4 and from the model name itself) |
| Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct ("Qwen") | "an emerging model that has gained increasing attention in the field of artificial intelligence since early 2025" | **7B** (from Table S4 and from the model name itself) |

Runs per model: baseline condition = **100 independent simulations per model per domain** (risk preference, social preference), each simulation = 25 rounds/budget-set decisions. Every experimental variation (persona, dialogue type, answer type, temperature, incentive, example) also runs **100 simulations per model per condition**. Case Study 2 (behavioral games) collects **100 valid responses per model per scenario** (5 scenarios).

**How the paper itself draws the large/small line — use their words, not an imported category.** The string "flagship" **does not appear anywhere in the paper.** The paper never proprietary/open-source-codes this split either in a clean way — in fact DeepSeek-V3 is explicitly classified as **open-source** ("the open-sourced model with the largest parameter size," and placed under the "Open-sourced" heading in Table S4), so "open vs. closed source" is NOT the paper's dividing line between the stable and unstable pair. The one place the paper draws the boundary explicitly is in its conclusion (line 1157):

> "the single-turn dialogue type reduces the economic rationality of two open-source models with small parameter sizes, Llama and Qwen, but not for the others, GPT and DeepSeek."

So the paper's own operative language is **"open-source models with small parameter sizes" (Llama, Qwen) vs. "the others" (GPT, DeepSeek)** — a distinction drawn on parameter count (7–8B vs. an undisclosed/671B pair), not on any "flagship" branding concept. A prior summary's use of "flagship" for GPT-4o/DeepSeek-V3 is an imported label, not the paper's own.

## (b) The size of the "essentially unmoved" claim — all four models, all conditions, with deltas and p-values

All CCEI values below are the paper's own reported means; deltas are experimental-condition value minus baseline value; p-values are the paper's FDR-corrected two-sample t-test p-values (see part (c) for the test/correction description) unless noted.

### Baseline CCEI (multi-turn, open-ended, no persona) — Case Study 1

| Model | Risk domain | Social domain |
|---|---|---|
| GPT | 1.000 | 0.994 |
| DeepSeek | 1.000 | 0.999 |
| Llama | 0.953 | 0.968 |
| Qwen | 0.980 | 0.994 |

(Quote, line 575: "The average CCEI scores of GPT, DeepSeek, Llama, and Qwen are 1.000, 1.000, 0.953, and 0.980 in the risk preference domain, and 0.994, 0.999, 0.968, and 0.994 in the social preference domain.")

### Dialogue-type manipulation: multi-turn (baseline) → single-turn

| Model | Risk: baseline → single-turn | Δ (risk) | p (risk) | Social: baseline → single-turn | Δ (social) | p (social) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT | 1.000 → 0.999 | −0.001 | p > 0.1 | 0.994 → 1.000 | **+0.006** | **p < 0.01** |
| DeepSeek | 1.000 → 1.000 | 0.000 (to 3 dp) | **p < 0.01** | 0.999 → 1.000 | +0.001 | **p < 0.01** |
| Llama | 0.953 → 0.841 | **−0.112** | p < 0.01 | 0.968 → 0.756 | **−0.212** | p < 0.01 |
| Qwen | 0.980 → 0.739 | **−0.241** | p < 0.01 | 0.994 → 0.889 | **−0.105** | p < 0.01 |

Direct quote (lines 849–871): "the single-turn dialogue approach does not substantially affect the CCEI scores of GPT (risk: 0.999 vs. 1.000, p>0.1, social: 1.000 vs. 0.994, p<0.01) and DeepSeek (risk: 1.000 vs. 1.000, p<0.01, social: 1.000 vs. 0.999, p<0.01). However, when using the single-turn dialogue, the CCEI of Llama decreases from 0.953 to 0.841 in the risk preference and from 0.968 to 0.756 in the social preference; that of Qwen decreases from 0.980 to 0.739 in the risk preference and from 0.994 to 0.889 in the social preference (all p<0.01)."

Overall sensitivity score for this manipulation: **λ(S) = 75.0%** (i.e., 6 of 8 model×domain cells were significant at p<0.05 after FDR correction).

**Important nuance the prior summary erased**: 3 of the 4 GPT/DeepSeek cells are formally **statistically significant** (p<0.01) despite the paper calling the effect "not substantial." The magnitudes are tiny (0.001–0.006 absolute CCEI points) against a baseline near the 1.000 ceiling with N=100 runs giving very high power — so "significant" here is a magnitude-blind label. The paper's own qualitative judgment ("does not substantially affect") is doing real interpretive work that the bare p-values don't capture on their own; conversely, for GPT/DeepSeek the deltas are real, not exactly zero, and one (DeepSeek risk) is significant even though the reported means round to identical 3-decimal values.

### Answer-type manipulation: open-ended (baseline) → multiple-choice (21 options, step of 5 points)

| Model | Risk: baseline → MC | Δ (risk) | p (risk) | Social: baseline → MC | Δ (social) | p (social) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GPT | 1.000 → 1.000 | 0.000 | p > 0.1 | 0.994 → 0.996 | +0.002 | p > 0.1 |
| DeepSeek | 1.000 → 1.000 | 0.000 | (not separately stated; grouped under "does not affect") | 0.999 → 0.994 | −0.005 | **p = 0.060** |
| Llama | 0.953 → 0.853 | **−0.100** | p < 0.01 | 0.968 → 0.936 | **−0.032** | p = 0.033 |
| Qwen | 0.980 → 0.902 | **−0.078** | p < 0.01 | 0.994 → 0.916 | **−0.078** | p < 0.01 |

Direct quote (lines 883–894): "compared to the baseline condition using the open-ended answer type, the multiple-choice answer type does not affect the CCEI scores for GPT (risk: 1.000 vs. 1.000, social: 0.996 vs. 0.994, p>0.1) and DeepSeek (risk: 1.000 vs. 1.000, social: 0.994 vs. 0.999, p=0.060). In contrast, under the multiple-choice answer type, the average CCEI scores in both domains decrease significantly for Llama (risk: 0.853 vs. 0.953, p<0.01, social: 0.936 vs. 0.968, p=0.033) and Qwen (risk: 0.902 vs. 0.980, social: 0.916 vs. 0.994, both p<0.01)."

Note: DeepSeek's social p=0.060 is **not** significant at the conventional α=0.05 (after FDR correction), so it's the one case where a small numeric drop (−0.005) is also non-significant — closer to genuinely "unmoved" than the dialogue-type case above.

### Is there a formal test of the null that GPT/DeepSeek's deltas are zero?

Yes, in the narrow sense that every comparison above (GPT, DeepSeek included) is run through the same two-sample t-test / FDR machinery as Llama and Qwen — it is not merely a visual/descriptive claim for the small models only. The paper's own text explicitly reports p-values for GPT and DeepSeek in every case (p>0.1, p<0.01, p=0.060 as tabulated above), so the "GPT/DeepSeek unmoved" claim is tested, not asserted by eyeballing. However — as flagged above — three of those tests for GPT/DeepSeek actually **reject** the null of zero difference (p<0.01) despite tiny effect sizes; the paper's "not substantially affected" language is a magnitude judgment layered on top of the significance tests, not a report of non-significance across the board.

### Persona and temperature (context, not the two/two split)

- Persona: "the rationality of all four models is not significantly influenced by the persona (all p>0.1), leading to a sensitivity score λ(S) of 0." (line ~830) — this includes Llama/Qwen, not just GPT/DeepSeek. Persona is genuinely a null result for everyone.
- Temperature (0–1 sweep): "We do not observe any significant effect of temperature on CCEI scores in both risk and social preference domains (all p>0.1)." (Appendix C)
- Incentive removal: "Removing incentive slightly reduces the CCEI of GPT by 0.004 in risk preference (p=0.023). Although statistically significant, the difference is relatively small in magnitude." — another case of a tiny-but-significant GPT effect, this time explicitly acknowledged by the authors as such.

## (c) Test-retest reliability vs. format sensitivity — DO NOT CONFLATE

**This paper measures format sensitivity, not test-retest reliability, for the CCEI outcome — and it does not report a within-format, across-run dispersion statistic (no SD, variance, ICC, CV, or CI is ever attached to a single model×format's CCEI distribution) in the parts of the text extracted here.**

Detail:

1. **What "100 simulations" buys you.** Each of the 100 runs per model per domain per condition IS a repeated draw of the *same* model under the *same* format (same dialogue type, same answer type, same persona/no-persona, same temperature). In principle this is exactly the data needed to compute a test-retest dispersion measure (e.g., SD of the 100 CCEI draws within the baseline condition for a given model). **But the paper never reports that number.** Every place CCEI is reported, only the **mean** across the 100 runs is given (e.g., "the average CCEI scores of GPT, DeepSeek, Llama, and Qwen are 1.000, 1.000, 0.953, and 0.980..."). No SD, variance, ICC, or coefficient of variation for the within-condition CCEI distribution appears anywhere in the extracted text.

2. **What the reported statistics actually test.** The paper's entire quantitative apparatus (two-sample t-tests, FDR correction, the "sensitivity score" λ(S)) is built to compare the **mean CCEI of the same model under condition A vs. condition B** (e.g., multi-turn vs. single-turn; open-ended vs. multiple-choice). That is a **format-sensitivity** test — different administration format, same model — not a test-retest reliability estimate (same format, repeated). The within-condition variance across the 100 runs is used internally to build the standard error for that t-test (and for the 95%-CI error bars in Figure 2/S4), but it is never surfaced as a standalone reliability statistic.

3. **One dispersion measure does exist, but it is not for CCEI and not in Case Study 1.** In Case Study 2 (dictator/ultimatum/public-goods/bomb-risk games), the paper computes a "normalized standard deviation" of the raw decision variable (dollar/point allocation) across the 100 baseline-condition runs per model per scenario:

   > "we calculate the normalized standard deviation of each LLM using σ̃ᵢ = Σₘ σᵢₘ / (Lₘ|M|)... The normalized standard deviations σ̃ᵢ for GPT, DeepSeek, Llama, and Qwen are 0.054, 0.030, 0.218, and 0.110, respectively. For comparison, the corresponding value for human decisions is 0.231." (line 1023–1069)

   This IS a genuine within-format, across-run dispersion statistic — it shows GPT/DeepSeek cluster tightly on a single answer across repeated (same-format) runs while Llama/Qwen spread more widely, similar to human variability. But it (a) applies to Case Study 2's raw choice variable, not the CCEI rationality index from Case Study 1, and (b) is descriptive (no CI/significance test attached to the σ̃ᵢ values themselves), and (c) is not labeled or used by the authors as a "test-retest reliability" statistic — it's presented as evidence of "heterogeneity in preferences within these models."

4. **Statistical test and multiple-comparison correction used for the format comparisons.** Two-sided two-sample t-tests throughout (footnote 6: "We employ two-sided tests throughout all analyses. Unless otherwise specified, two-sample t-tests are used in this paper."), with **false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini-Hochberg, citation [5])** applied within each "family" of tests. For the dialogue-type and answer-type manipulations specifically: "for the dialogue type and answer type, similar analysis is conducted in 8 tests (4 models × 2 preferences)" — i.e., each variation's 8 p-values (4 models × 2 domains) are FDR-corrected together as one family. Persona uses a larger family (112 tests: 4 models × 2 preferences × 14 conditions).

**Bottom line for (c): the paper is a same-model, cross-format comparison (format sensitivity) with a formal significance test on the mean CCEI shift; it is NOT a same-model, same-format repeated-measurement study reporting a reliability coefficient (no ICC, no CV, no SD of the CCEI distribution itself). Any claim that this paper "establishes test-retest reliability" for GPT-4o/DeepSeek-V3 is not supported by what is reported — the paper supports a (tested) claim about format sensitivity only.**

## (d) Design details

- **Budget sets/rounds per session**: 25 rounds (budget-set decisions) per simulation, in both the risk-preference and social-preference domains. ("We construct 25 rounds of tasks in each simulation, with budget lines that vary between rounds.")
- **Goods per domain**: 2. Risk domain = allocate 100 points between "Asset A" and "Asset B" (i.e., two accounts/state-contingent claims, exchange rates differ, 50% probability of payoff from each). Social domain = allocate 100 points between self and a randomly matched other agent, with distinct exchange rates for the two parties.
- **How prices/exchange rates were drawn, and to what precision**: The prompt template shows a placeholder — "In this round, investing every 1 point for Asset A returns {} dollars, and investing every 1 point for Asset B returns {} dollars" — with the note "{} represents the randomly generated number in each round." **The paper's visible text does not state the distribution used to draw these numbers nor their decimal precision** (e.g., it does not say "two decimals" or give a range). This appears to be inherited from the original task design (Chen et al., cited as [23]/[24]) but is not restated in this paper's main text or in the extracted appendix prompt sections.
- **Are budgets exhausted?** Yes — full allocation is enforced/required. Footnote 2 defines an answer as invalid if "the total points allocated to two accounts do not sum to 100," meaning the 100-point budget must be fully spent for the answer to count.
- **Elicitation formats, exact names/descriptions**:
  - *Multi-turn dialogue* (baseline): "each round of question is asked separately, with the preceding dialogue included... each LLM makes one decision per round."
  - *Single-turn dialogue* (manipulation): "a single-turn dialogue format that incorporates all 25 tasks in one prompt, instructing LLMs to make 25 decisions simultaneously."
  - *Open-ended answer type* (baseline): free-form numeric JSON response, no predefined options ("Open-ended answer type prompts responses without predefined options").
  - *Multiple-choice answer type* (manipulation): "the continuous budget set is discretized into 21 allocation options with a step size of 5 points, and LLMs are required to select one option for each task" (i.e., 0, 5, 10, ..., 100 point allocations to Asset/party A).
  - Also tested (not part of the "unmoved" claim but part of the 8-tactic framework): persona assignment (14 conditions: gender, age, education, ethnicity per Chen et al., plus occupations per Mei et al.), temperature sweep (0 to 1, step 0.1), incentive removal and stake-size scaling (×10, ×100, ×1000), and presence/absence of the illustrative example.
- **Sample-size / power justification for N=100**: "By assuming equal variance and specifying the minimum detectable difference in CCEI scores... of 0.01, we use the standard deviation obtained in Chen et al. [23] and compute the minimum sample size for each condition, which is 24 in the risk preference and 18 in the social preference, respectively." — 100 simulations was chosen to exceed this minimum.

## (e) Bronars power / predictive success / random-choice benchmark / power analysis

- **Random-choice benchmark: Yes.** "To validate the sufficient power of detecting GARP violations, simulated CCEI is generated using random allocation across 25 decisions for 100 simulated agents [12]. The simulated CCEI is significantly lower than all four LLMs in both preference domains (all p<0.01)." Citation [12] in the reference list is **Bronars, Stephen G. (1987), "The power of nonparametric tests of preference maximization," Econometrica, 55(3): 693–698** — i.e., this is indeed a Bronars-style power check by method and citation, but note: **the word "Bronars" itself never appears in the paper's main or appendix body text** (it only appears in the reference list entry). Calling it "Bronars-style power" (as a prior summary did) is an accurate methodological label the reader supplies, not the paper's own terminology.
- **No numeric value for the simulated/random CCEI itself is given** — only "significantly lower... p<0.01." No mean, SD, or exact CCEI figure for the 100 simulated random agents is stated in the extracted text.
- **Predictive success (Selten-style)**: Not used or mentioned anywhere in the extracted text.
- **Formal power analysis for sample size**: Yes — see (d) above (minimum detectable difference of 0.01 in CCEI, using Chen et al.'s SD, yielding minimum N of 24 (risk) / 18 (social); actual N used = 100, i.e., over-powered relative to the stated minimum).
- **Human benchmark comparison (not a power test, but a related sanity check)**: "In human experiments using comparable budgetary tasks, the average CCEI ranges from 0.881 to 0.980 in the risk preference..., and from 0.86 to 0.967 in the social preference..." and a Turing-test-style resampling comparison (10,000 draws) shows "all four LLMs pass the Turing test... our randomly simulated agents fail the Turing test."

## What this paper does and does not establish

This paper establishes **format sensitivity** — same model, different elicitation format (dialogue type, answer type) — with formal two-sample t-tests, FDR-corrected across each family of 8 comparisons (4 models × 2 domains). Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B show large, significant CCEI drops under single-turn or multiple-choice formats (up to −0.241); GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V3 show much smaller shifts, three of which are nonetheless statistically significant (p<0.01) despite being 0.001–0.006 in magnitude. It does **not** establish test-retest reliability: no SD, variance, ICC, or CV is reported for the CCEI distribution within a fixed model×format across the 100 repeated runs. The one within-format dispersion statistic in the paper (normalized SD of raw choices) belongs to Case Study 2, not the CCEI. The paper also never uses the word "flagship"; its own operative split is "open-source models with small parameter sizes" (Llama, Qwen) vs. "GPT and DeepSeek," a distinction by parameter count, not proprietary status — DeepSeek-V3 is itself open-source.
